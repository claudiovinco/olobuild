Inside the budget deal that almost didn't happen

By Elena Marchetti6 March 202612 min read

The talks ran past midnight three nights in a row before a deal emerged.

For seventy-two hours, the negotiations looked finished. Aides drifted out of the building, briefings were cancelled, and the wire services began drafting the story of a collapse. Then, sometime after eleven on the third night, a single concession on housing changed everything — and with it, the shape of the coalition.

What follows is a reconstruction, built from conversations with a dozen people in the room, of how a deal that should have been routine came within an hour of falling apart, and what the final compromise will mean for the year ahead.

“Nobody wanted to be the one to walk. So we all stayed, and we all got slightly less than we came for.”

The week the maths stopped working

By Tuesday, the spreadsheet that had anchored every meeting no longer balanced. A late revision to the revenue forecast opened a gap that neither side was willing to close on the other's terms. For two days the conversation circled the same three numbers without landing.

The breakthrough, when it came, was almost accidental — a junior adviser's offhand suggestion that the housing allocation could be phased rather than cut. It was the kind of idea that survives only because everyone is too tired to object.

What the compromise actually does

In practical terms, the deal trades a faster timeline for a smaller first-year commitment. Supporters call it pragmatic; critics call it a delay dressed as a decision. Both are, in their way, correct. The real test arrives next spring, when the phased numbers come due and the easy part is over.
